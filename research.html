Working papers

The Effects of Advertising and Personalization on Search Costs: Evidence from an Online Field Experiment

Job Market Paper

with Cristobal Cheyre and Alessandro Acquisti

Abstract

We study the impact of advertising and personalization on consumer search costs using data from an online field experiment where participants browse with either advertising blocked, or with behaviorally targeted advertising, or with reduced tracking that limits ad personalization. We introduce a method to identify and analyze shopping intents using browsing and platforms' metadata. Relative to participants who see no advertising, those exposed to targeted advertising spend 24 percent more time per product evaluated and 30 percent more time per shopping intent. Yet they evaluate no more products, visit no more merchants, and purchase products of similar price and quality. Participants exposed to less personalized advertising are statistically indistinguishable from those who see no ads on these outcomes. We then evaluate whether the additional time spent under targeting reflects more intensive product evaluation, using a duration-based search model to estimate how quickly time spent on a product improves the consumer's information about it. Under targeted advertising, this learning rate is about one-third lower than when advertising is blocked, and the time needed to acquire the same information about a product is about twice as high. Thus, the additional search time generated by targeting appears to be less productive rather than more informative. Valuing this additional attention at the opportunity cost of time implies a consumer cost of approximately $15 to $41 per participant over three months.

Consent and Choice: Experimental Evidence on User Behavior at Consent Banners

with Francesco Decarolis, Cristobal Cheyre, and Alessandro Acquisti

CEPR WP

Abstract

Cookie banners appear on websites when visitors first arrive, informing them about a site's use of cookies and tracking technologies, and often asking them whether they consent to their data being collected and shared with third parties. We use a panel of 780 U.S. Chrome users, whose online behaviors were tracked by a browser extension they kept installed for up to three months, to investigate how users interact with cookie banners in the wild, over time, across all the websites they visit. We find that users ignore two in three banners they encounter, and engage with banners mostly on websites that force a response; these websites register three times more banner engagement than the ones where banners can be ignored, but lose more than twice as many visitors after they encounter the banner. Users' explicit choices (accepting or rejecting tracking) exhibit across-user heterogeneity, and are significantly influenced by whether a one-click reject option is offered. We set a single signal from the choices users make during the first third of the days we observe them, and find that this signal is consistent with about two thirds of their later encounters. We calculate that users would spend 58 percent less time on banners if websites could not force a response and engagement on these websites fell to the level observed where banners can be ignored.

Cursed by the Algorithm: Learning from Behavior That Does Not Reveal Welfare

Solo-authored

SSRN

Abstract

Firms often learn what to offer customers from what they take. I ask what happens when some of that take-up is compulsive. An intermediary chooses how many items of each category to offer and treats consumption as taste, but cannot see when a vulnerable agent enters a "hot" state in which their triggering category looks more valuable than on reflection. Agents who never enter the hot state get what they would choose on reflection, whereas vulnerable agents are offered ever more of their triggering category until their welfare falls and they leave. Starting from low vulnerability, this spiral starts only if the agent already likes that category enough. Capping the slots of any one category, or requiring a minimum number of slots for several categories, raises vulnerable agents' welfare at a cost to agents with narrow tastes, without identifying who is vulnerable, and so does a shorter feed, at a cost to agents who use the platform heavily, provided users cannot override it. In simulations of social media, users with a moderate interest in the triggering category are harmed most. With the algorithms that platforms use and tastes observed on a short-video platform, the spiral and its threshold reappear, and the cap protects vulnerable users at almost no cost to others. An informed intermediary would keep vulnerable agents just above the point at which they leave, so information alone does not protect them, whereas the type-blind rules do.

Beyond Search: LLM Adoption and Web Traffic Concentration

Best Student Paper Award, WISE 2025

with Samira Gholami, Cristobal Cheyre, and Alessandro Acquisti

PDF

Abstract

Search engines have long served as the primary gateway to the web, dispersing user attention across a wide range of external websites. The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs), which provide answers directly within platform interfaces, raises concerns that online activity may become more centralized. We study whether LLM adoption displaces traditional web navigation or instead restructures how users discover and consume information. Using nationally representative Comscore panel data from 2019–2024 and session-level evidence from the Internet Behavior Experiment, we examine how search activity, web exploration, and downstream navigation evolve with LLM use. Exploiting staggered adoption across users, we find that LLM adoption coincides with a sustained increase in the number of unique websites visited, alongside a short-lived rise in traditional search activity, suggesting that LLMs can complement rather than replace search. Session-level analyses show that most LLM use occurs alongside search, while LLM-only sessions remain rare. Moreover, when LLMs do route users to external websites, the resulting traffic is no more concentrated than traffic generated by traditional search.

Inattention or Avoidance? A Field Infrastructure for Testing Nutritional Labels Online

NET Institute Working Paper

with Camilla Cherubini

Abstract

We build a field infrastructure to test how nutritional information should be displayed online. Its core is a browser extension that changes what Walmart.com shows next to each product and records what shoppers look at and buy, in their own accounts and with their own money. We use it to ask whether shoppers who leave nutritional information unread do so because reading it costs effort or because they would rather not know. In a pilot with 109 U.S. households, the extension shows participants nothing, a 0–100 nutritional score, the score with the nutrients and additives behind it, or the same label behind one click. We find that participants leave closed 96 percent of the labels one click away, and that they open least often the labels of products that score poorly and that they already buy. Participants who see the score alone fill their carts like those who see nothing. Participants who also see the nutrients and additives choose products that score 4.5 points higher within the same type, about 7 percent of the average score, and are 10 percentage points less likely to buy a product rated poor, while participants who have the same label behind one click do not. Participants attribute the same value to keeping the extension in every condition. We read these patterns as preliminary, since they come from a small pilot. The infrastructure could offer an efficient way to test health policy interventions, such as the design of nutritional labels, in real purchases and before they are adopted.

An Experimental Infrastructure for Ecologically Valid Studies of Online Advertising, Tracking, and Targeting

with Cristobal Cheyre, Li Jiang, Florian Schaub, Zijun Ding, Yucheng Li, and Alessandro Acquisti

Abstract

This paper introduces an experimental methodology for studying the impacts of online advertising, tracking, and targeting on users. The infrastructure deploys a client–server architecture that enables randomized field experiments by assigning participants to ad-blocking, anti-tracking, or full ad-exposure conditions, capturing rich longitudinal data across browsers, email, and mobile devices while preserving ecological validity. By combining fine-grained behavioral measures with repeated surveys, it provides a rigorous framework for causal inference on consumer welfare, information-seeking behavior, and privacy in digital advertising markets.

Work in progress

SafeFeeds

with Miguel Risco

Abstract

We study the impact of education and algorithm reset on adolescents' well-being. Social-media recommender systems learn from engagement and can concentrate attention on a narrow set of content, with particular risks for younger users. In a field experiment, we test two low-cost interventions: a digital-literacy component that helps teenagers understand how their feeds are curated, and a reset of the recommendation algorithm that clears its learned profile. We measure how each intervention affects feed composition, time on platform, and self-reported well-being, and whether such uniformly applied tools can improve adolescent outcomes without identifying which users are most at risk.

Policy work

Making Data Protection Fit for the Age of AI

CERRE, Centre on Regulation in Europe, 2026

with Marco Bassini

PDF

Abstract

This CERRE report analyzes the EU's proposed Digital Omnibus and the reform of the GDPR in the age of artificial intelligence, combining legal and economic perspectives. It argues that data protection and AI innovation are not inherently in conflict: in many settings the interests of users and developers are aligned, and the challenge is a framework that does not distinguish the cases where they converge from those where they genuinely diverge. The report assesses key provisions, from the notion of personal data to legitimate interest for AI training and consent management, and proposes a phased path toward a more proportionate, risk-based regime.

Unlocking Growth: The Economic Effects of GDPR in Europe

Clifford Chance, 2025

with Francesco Decarolis

Abstract

This report studies the economic effects of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) on firm behavior, investment, and innovation across European countries. Bringing together cross-country evidence and institutional variation in enforcement and compliance, it documents the costs GDPR has imposed on firms, including lower profits and reduced investment, and argues for a more proportionate, risk-based approach to data regulation.
